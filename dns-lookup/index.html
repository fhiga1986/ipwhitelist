What is a DNS lookup?

A DNS (Domain Name System) lookup translates a human-readable domain name like github.com into machine-readable records — most commonly an IP address. Every time you visit a website, your browser performs a DNS lookup behind the scenes. This tool lets you query DNS records directly, bypassing local caches for real-time results.

The query goes to Google Public DNS over HTTPS (dns.google), a recursive resolver that fetches the answer from the domain's authoritative name servers. That makes it ideal for checking a record you have just changed: your laptop's cache and your ISP's resolver may still hold the old value, while a fresh recursive query shows what the rest of the world is about to see.

How to read the results

  • TTL is how many seconds a resolver may cache the answer. A record with TTL 300 can be changed and seen worldwide within about five minutes; TTL 86400 may take a full day.
  • MX records show priority target, for example 10 aspmx.l.google.com.. If several records share a priority, mail is spread across them.
  • TXT records are returned in quotes and may be split into 255-character chunks that the receiving server joins back together — long DKIM keys always look like this.
  • CNAME chains are followed automatically: the answer may list the CNAME first and then the A records of the final target.
  • No records found is a valid answer (NODATA): the name exists but has no record of that type. NXDOMAIN means the name does not exist at all.

What is DNS propagation and how long does it take?

DNS propagation is the time it takes for changes to DNS records to spread across all DNS servers worldwide. When you update a DNS record — such as changing your A record to a new server IP — DNS resolvers around the world continue serving the old record until their cache expires based on the record's TTL (Time To Live) value.

Propagation typically takes between a few minutes and 48 hours. If you need faster propagation, lower your TTL to 300 seconds (5 minutes) at least 24 hours before making the change, then revert to a higher TTL after the change is confirmed live. Changing name servers (NS) at the registrar is the slowest case, because the parent zone's TTL — often 48 hours for .com — is outside your control.

Email DNS: SPF, DKIM and DMARC in one minute

For email deliverability, always verify your MX, SPF (TXT record), DKIM (TXT record) and DMARC (TXT record) are correctly configured. Missing or misconfigured email DNS records are the most common cause of email going to spam.

  • SPF lives in a TXT record on the domain itself and lists who may send mail for it: v=spf1 include:_spf.google.com -all. Keep it under 10 DNS lookups.
  • DKIM publishes a public key at selector._domainkey.example.com so receivers can verify the signature your mail server adds.
  • DMARC lives at _dmarc.example.com and tells receivers what to do when SPF/DKIM fail: v=DMARC1; p=quarantine; rua=mailto:dmarc@example.com.

FAQ

DNS lookup questions

Your local resolver may have cached outdated records, or your ISP may intercept DNS queries. Google's DoH API queries authoritative nameservers directly, giving you the most current, accurate results — which matters when you've just updated a DNS record and want to verify propagation.
DNS changes typically propagate globally within 15–60 minutes when TTL values are low. However, if your previous record had a high TTL (e.g. 86400 seconds = 24 hours), caches across the internet may hold the old value for up to that long. Name-server changes at the registrar can take up to 48 hours. This tool bypasses caching and shows the current authoritative answer.
TTL (Time To Live) is the number of seconds a DNS record can be cached by resolvers before they must re-query the authoritative nameserver. A lower TTL means faster propagation of changes but more DNS queries. Typical values range from 300 (5 minutes) to 86400 (24 hours).
An A record maps a domain directly to an IPv4 address. A CNAME creates an alias pointing one domain to another, and inherits whatever records the target has. CNAMEs cannot coexist with other records at the same name, which is why they are not allowed on the root domain (@) — use your DNS provider's ALIAS or ANAME record there instead.
Look up the TXT records of your bare domain. Exactly one of them must start with v=spf1, list every service that sends mail for you (include: entries or ip4: ranges) and end with -all (reject) or ~all (soft fail). Two SPF records, or more than 10 nested lookups, make the record invalid and receivers ignore it.
